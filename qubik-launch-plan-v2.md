## Why wholesale distributors

I went into this assignment with a view. I worked with ⟦role⟧ at Cometlab.ai, which sells similar AI and automation services to Indian businesses. The buyers who actually came looking for this kind of help were manufacturers and distributors ⟦add one real example in your words: what they asked for, what was built⟧. Their business runs on documents, and the documents keep piling up as they grow.

Before trusting that in a different country, I tested it against Australian data and the brief:

- **There are enough of them.** ABS counts about 10,800 wholesale businesses (excluding commission agents) turning over A$2–10M. I estimate about 6,700 are in the A$3–10M range. That's 16% of all wholesalers, against 7% for trades [1]. Meta can't target by company size, so a higher share in the band means more of the people who reply are worth a call.
- **Their problem is the kind current AI is good at.** Reading purchase orders, supplier invoices and shipping documents and putting the data in the right place is mature, low-risk work for Qubik's automation specialist. I looked at trades and job-shop manufacturers too. There, the hard part is quoting from drawings, which is a riskier first build.
- **An accountant sees it first.** Supplier bills, landed cost, GST on imports and stock values all end up in the Xero file Qubik already reads every month. Qubik is a Xero Platinum partner [3]. A generic AI agency can't claim that.
- **The pressure on them is real and current.** The ACCC found stevedores charging record prices, with landside charges now about half of their revenue, and says those costs flow through to importers [5]. Freight, port charges and the dollar all move. Every move should change a sell price, and in most businesses this size that happens late.

I also considered trades, pubs and clubs, labour hire and job-shop manufacturers. I set them aside for a lower share in the band, a crowded offer, thin margins, or a harder first build.

## 1. Buyer and problem

**The organisation.** A business that buys from overseas factories, holds stock in an Australian warehouse, and sells to trade or retail customers: hardware, electrical, lighting, auto parts, packaging, homewares, food. Revenue A$3–10M, usually 10–40 staff. They wouldn't call themselves "importer-distributors". They say "we're the Australian distributor for…" or "we wholesale to the trade". The ads speak to what they do, not a label.

**The decision-maker.** The owner or MD, often the person who built the supplier relationships. The office or finance manager, or an external bookkeeper, has a big say because they live with the problem.

**What their week looks like.** Orders arrive all day as emails, PDFs, phone calls and the odd photo of a handwritten list. Someone types each one into Cin7, Unleashed, MYOB or Xero, matching the customer's names for products to the business's own codes. When a container lands, someone else gathers the supplier invoice, freight bill, customs broker's invoice and the duty and GST, and works out what each product really cost. That's usually done in a spreadsheet, often weeks later. Meanwhile customers ring to ask where their back-order is, and a person has to go and look.

**What it costs (my estimate, to be replaced by real numbers in the diagnostic).**
- **Order entry.** A A$6M distributor handling about 150 orders a week at five or six minutes each spends 12–15 hours a week on order entry alone. That's most of a part-time role. Then there are wrong items, wrong prices and the credit notes that follow.
- **Late container costing.** This costs more and is harder to see. If costs rise 2% and prices follow a quarter later, that's around A$30k of margin gone on A$1.5M of sales.
- **Total.** Together, comfortably over A$100k a year for a business this size.

**Buying triggers.**
- A freight or exchange-rate jump.
- An order-entry person leaving.
- A stocktake that doesn't reconcile.
- Growth that means hiring another office person.
- A bad quarter where the margin "disappeared".
- The calendar: Christmas containers land in October and November. With Chinese New Year on 6 February 2027 [7], pre-holiday orders go to factories in November. Right now is when the paperwork peaks.

**Ability to pay.** At A$3–10M with stock on the floor, an A$57–90k project is a normal capex decision if it pays back within a year. That's the test the diagnostic exists to answer. Cash position is checked in qualification.

**Why Meta can reach them.** These owners use Facebook personally, like most business owners in their 40s to 60s (an assumption I'd confirm with the free age breakdown in Ads Manager's reach estimate). Meta can reach them. What it can't do is identify them, because interests and job titles became loose suggestions in 2025 [6]. So the ad and the form do the filtering: a hook only a real importer recognises, and questions a dropshipper or employee can't pass.

## 2. Offer and proof

**The Container-to-Cash Diagnostic. A$3,500 + GST, founding-client price.** Three weeks, fixed scope. The client gets:

1. **The order check.** We sample two weeks of incoming orders and time how long they take to enter, how many arrive in a format a machine could read, and where mistakes happen.
2. **The container check.** We rebuild the landed cost of their last three to five shipments from the real documents. Then we compare it with the cost in their system and the price they sold at.
3. **An import tax tidy-up.** We check how duty and GST on imports flow through Xero, and whether the deferred GST scheme [4] would ease their cash flow. This is a quick, practical win only an accountant can hand over.
4. **A plan with a price.** The two or three automations worth building, ranked by payback, with a fixed quote for the first one.
5. **A one-hour sit-down with the owner** to walk through it, plus a short written report.

They give us read-only Xero access, a set of order emails and shipment documents, and about two hours of their team's time.

**On price.** Australian consumer law says a "was" price has to be one the service actually sold at [8]. Until the diagnostic has sold at A$7,500, I'd present it as a founding-client price: "A$3,500 for our first ten clients; A$7,500 after that." That's also true, and it gives a reason to act now.

**Proof I'd use at launch, all of it real:**
- Qubik's own standing: Xero Platinum partner since 2013, payroll certified, CA ANZ and CPA memberships [3] ⟦confirm with Qubik⟧.
- The automation specialist, named, showing something they've already built ⟦ask Qubik⟧.
- A sample diagnostic page made with dummy data and clearly labelled as such, so a buyer can see exactly what they'll get.
- The founding-client deal itself: the first ten get the low price, and in return Qubik can publish anonymised before-and-after numbers. That's how proof gets built without being invented.

**How the diagnostic leads to the project.** The diagnostic puts a dollar figure on the client's own problem. That figure is the business case for the implementation, which is already scoped and priced in the report. The A$3,500 is credited in full if they go ahead within 30 days. A typical first build:
- An assistant that reads incoming orders and drafts them in the client's system, so staff check rather than type.
- Automatic landed costing when a broker's invoice arrives, with a list of products whose margin has slipped below target.

The client's team approves anything the system isn't sure about, and their data stays in their own systems. The size of that build is what sets the A$57–90k range.

## 3. Meta acquisition

**Objective.** Leads, using an Instant Form set to "higher intent", which adds a review step. Outcomes go back from the CRM to Meta, so later spend can chase qualified leads rather than form fills. Facebook and Instagram feeds and Reels only. I'd leave Audience Network off, because in my experience that's where junk leads come from. Spend: A$900 over 14 days.

**Where to find them.** Generic B2B targeting won't find a lighting importer, so I'd use three signals that do:

1. **Where they work.** Distributors cluster in warehouse estates near the ports. Meta lets you drop location pins with a radius of about 1 mile [9]. For example:
   - **Brisbane:** Eagle Farm, Hemmant, Acacia Ridge, Brendale
   - **Sydney:** Wetherill Park, Smithfield, Moorebank
   - **Melbourne:** Dandenong South, Laverton North, Truganina
   - **Perth:** Kewdale

   Pins catch staff as well as owners, but they shift the mix towards businesses that hold stock.
2. **When it hurts.** Run the test in late October and November, when Christmas stock is landing and pre-Chinese New Year orders are due. The same message will work better then than in March.
3. **Where they gather.** Exhibitors at trade fairs are almost all importers and wholesalers. AGHA's Gifted Melbourne (30 Jan–1 Feb 2027) and the Reed Gift Fair in Sydney (February 2027) are worth geofencing during show days [10, dates to confirm]. That falls after the first 14 days, so it's the month-three play.

Later, with consent checked: lookalikes built from Qubik's own importer clients, and retargeting people who watched the video. Qubik's existing clients are excluded from the start.

**Creative.** A real Qubik accountant on camera, filmed on a phone in a client's warehouse or beside a desk of shipping paperwork. Plain and specific. The angle is the gap owners already feel but rarely measure: costs went up, prices didn't, and nobody noticed for a quarter.

**First test.** Same ad, two audiences: **broad Australia vs warehouse-estate pins**, about A$450 each. I'd judge it on cost per qualified lead, not cost per lead. Four to six leads won't prove anything statistically. What the test will show is whether the pins send better-fit people, which decides how the next A$5,000 gets spent.

## 4. Qualification, nurture and handover

**Scoring, out of 100.**
- **Revenue:** A$5–10M = 25, A$2–5M = 20, over A$10M = 15
- **Role:** owner/GM = 20, finance/office = 12
- **Imports:** by container = 15, smaller shipments = 10
- **Orders typed in by hand:** mostly = 15, a mix = 8
- **Order volume:** 200+ = 10, 50–200 = 6
- **Funding:** yes = 15, possibly = 8

Revenue under A$2M, or a role of "Other", means no sales call. 70 or above goes straight to a booking link. 45–69 gets a person looking at it. Below 45 goes to a slow monthly email.

**Six events from form to sales:**

| # | Event | What happens | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead arrives** | Lands in the CRM within a minute. Phone and email cleaned, duplicates merged, score set, consent recorded, source ad tagged. | Automation |
| 2 | **First reply, within 5 minutes** | Qualified: SMS and email from a named Qubik accountant with a booking link. Borderline: a person reviews it the same day. Low score: an educational email, no sales time. | Automation, with Marketing's copy |
| 3 | **Check the unclear ones** | One personal message asking the one thing we don't know (usually revenue or role). Re-scored on the reply. | Marketing |
| 4 | **Booking** | Booking a 20-minute call stops every chaser instantly. Three quick pre-call questions: system used, orders a week, last container's arrival date. Not booked: one email on day 1, an SMS on day 3, a last email on day 6, then monthly. | Automation |
| 5 | **Reminders, reschedules, no-shows** | Email the day before, SMS two hours before, both with a reschedule link. A no-show gets a "sorry we missed you" SMS ten minutes after, an email the next day, and a phone call on day 3, then moves to monthly email. | Automation, phone call by Sales |
| 6 | **Handover** | Sales gets a one-page brief: their answers, which ad they saw, every message so far. Sales accepts or rejects within one business day and says why. Rejection reasons come back to me weekly and change the form or the ads. | Sales, with Marketing |

**The rules that stop it going wrong:**
- **Duplicates.** Matched on mobile, email or company. Merged, never restarted. An existing Qubik client is routed to their own accountant. An open deal goes to the rep who owns it.
- **Replies.** Any reply pauses the automation. AI can sort replies and suggest an answer. A person sends anything beyond logistics, within two business hours.
- **Opt-outs.** STOP or unsubscribe takes them out of every channel straight away. The law allows five working days [11]. Every message says it's from Qubik.
- **Uncertain answers.** "Possibly", or a revenue of A$2–5M, never gets auto-rejected. A person looks.
- **Escalation to a human, fast.** A score of 85 or more (call within the hour), a complaint, a privacy question, a pricing negotiation, or anything the automation fails on.

**Who owns what.**
- **Marketing (me):** ads, form, scoring, copy, nurture, weekly numbers, and the feedback loop with sales.
- **Automation (Qubik's specialist and IT):** CRM connections, routing, SMS and email sending, consent records, sending outcomes back to Meta.
- **Sales (Qubik):** the calls, accepting or rejecting leads on time, logging outcomes, closing the diagnostic and the project.

## 5. Economics and downside

Using the brief's rates, all applied to qualified leads, and A$73.5k as the midpoint implementation value:

| | A$150 CPL | A$225 CPL |
|---|---|---|
| Leads (A$900 ÷ CPL) | 6 | 4 |
| Qualified (30%) | 1.8 | 1.2 |
| Attended meetings (70% of qualified) | 1.26 | 0.84 |
| Diagnostics sold (50% of qualified) | 0.9 = A$3,150 | 0.6 = A$2,100 |
| Implementations (25% of qualified) | 0.45 = A$33,075 | 0.3 = A$22,050 |
| **Gross attributed revenue** | **A$36,225** | **A$24,150** |
| Range at A$57–90k | A$28,800–43,650 | A$19,200–29,100 |

If the diagnostic fee is credited against implementations, take off about A$1,600 and A$1,050 respectively.

**What I'd tell Qubik straight.**
- **A$900 buys four to six leads.** The real result will be either nothing or one sale. It's a test of whether the funnel works, not a revenue forecast.
- **The brief's rates are generous.** Selling a diagnostic to 50% of qualified leads when only 70% turn up means 7 in 10 attendees buy. Implementing with 25% means half of diagnostic clients go on to a project.
- **Almost all the revenue is implementations**, and that cash arrives two to four months after the diagnostic.

**The main risk is lead quality, not lead cost.** At the brief's rates each lead is worth about A$6,000, so even a A$600 CPL would work if the 30% qualification held. The danger is the form filling up with people who will never buy. That would push qualification down to 10–15%, and the whole chain shrinks with it.

**What I'd change first if it's below plan:** the filter, before anything else.
- **Qualified share low:** put revenue and "do you import?" first, sharpen the "for distributors who import" line, and add the review step if it isn't already doing its job.
- **Leads cost too much but the right people are coming:** change the hook and the video.
- **Right people, not booking:** speed and the call offer.
- **Booking but not buying:** the proof and the founding-client terms.
